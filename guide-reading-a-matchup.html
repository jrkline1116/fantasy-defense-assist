How to Read a D/ST Matchup

Updated October 14, 2026 · 5 min read

The opponent rank on our grid tells you what has happened. This guide covers why it happens, so you can spot a good matchup before the numbers catch up, or a bad one hiding behind a green cell.

Sacks: the steadiest points

Sacks are worth only one point each, but they come every week. They depend mostly on the offense: a weak offensive line, a quarterback who holds the ball, or a team that's trailing and has to throw. An offense that's allowed three or more sacks a game is usually a good streaming target, even if it scores some points.

Turnovers: the big swings

Interceptions and fumble recoveries are worth two points each, and they're how a defense gets to double digits. They're less predictable than sacks, but they cluster around certain offenses: young or struggling quarterbacks, offenses that throw a lot while behind, and teams with ball-security problems. A quarterback who has thrown interceptions in most of his starts is a better sign than one who threw four in a single game.

Points allowed: think about the offense's ceiling

Points-allowed bonuses reward holding an offense under about two touchdowns. A useful shortcut is the betting market: the game total and point spread together give each team an implied team total. As a rough rule, defenses facing an offense implied for about 20 points or fewer are in a good spot, and offenses implied for 27 or more are ones to avoid. Implied totals are already adjusted for injuries and matchups, which makes them a good check on season-long averages.

Quarterback changes

Nothing moves a matchup faster than a starting quarterback getting hurt. A backup, especially an inexperienced one, tends to take more sacks and throw more interceptions. Because our ranks are based on the season so far, they won't reflect a quarterback change right away. If a red or gray matchup suddenly features a backup quarterback, it may be better than it looks.

Weather and home field

Strong wind and heavy rain lower passing efficiency and scoring, and they tend to help defenses. Home defenses also do a little better on average, partly because crowd noise can cause false starts and rushed snaps. Neither is a reason to start a defense on its own, but either can break a tie between two similar options.

Putting it together

  1. Start with the grid's opponent rank to find the green matchups.
  2. Check the opponent's sacks allowed and turnovers in the game-by-game popup.
  3. Look at the implied team total, if you follow betting lines.
  4. Check the news for quarterback injuries and weather before you lock it in.
